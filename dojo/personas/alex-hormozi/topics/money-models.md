# Money Models

## When to Use
- You have one offer and want to turn it into a full revenue engine
- Your customer buys once and disappears
- Your ads barely work because average order value or lifetime value is too low
- You need customers to spend more money, faster, and repeatedly

## Fails When
- The core offer is not validated
- Fulfillment breaks on the first product and you add more offers anyway
- The added offers are random revenue grabs instead of natural next steps
- You skip the math and just copy someone else's funnel

## Core Concept

A money model is not "an offer." It is the deliberate sequence of offers around the customer. The Grand Slam Offer gets someone to buy. The money model asks: what do they buy before it, what do they buy after it, what do they buy instead of it, what do they keep buying, and what do they buy when they come back?

Most businesses have a transaction. Hormozi wants a machine. A transaction says: "Here is my product. Buy it." A money model says: "Here is the first easy step. Here is the main transformation. Here is the higher-value version. Here is the fallback if the big thing is too much. Here is the monthly continuation. Here is the winback when you leave."

The model matters because acquisition math depends on lifetime value. If you sell one $500 product and spend $300 to acquire a customer, you have no room for mistakes. If the same customer buys a $49 attraction offer, then a $500 core offer, then a $1,500 upsell, then a $99/month continuity offer, the exact same lead source can become wildly profitable.

The sequence has six common pieces:

**Attraction Offer.** A free or low-cost front-end offer that earns trust and creates a first transaction. It should solve a narrow problem and naturally reveal the bigger problem.

**Core Offer.** The main Grand Slam Offer. This is the center of the model. If this doesn't work, the rest is decoration.

**Upsell.** A higher-value add-on offered after the customer has committed. It increases average order value by helping the customer get the outcome faster, easier, or more completely.

**Downsell.** A lower-friction option when the customer says no to the core or upsell. It preserves the buyer instead of losing them entirely.

**Continuity.** A recurring offer that keeps delivering value over time. It stabilizes cash flow and increases lifetime value.

**Winback.** A re-entry offer for churned or inactive customers. It treats former customers as warm leads with history, not strangers.

The rule: every offer in the model must make sense from the customer's point of view. If the customer bought A, B should feel like the obvious next step. If they declined B, C should feel like a reasonable alternative. If they finished the program, continuity should feel like protection against backsliding.

## How to Apply

1. **Write the core offer first.** What is the main paid transformation? Who is it for? What result does it create? What price and guarantee make it a Grand Slam Offer?

2. **Map the customer journey.** Before the core offer, what smaller problem do they have? During the core offer, what friction do they hit? After the core offer, what new problem appears? After they leave, what pulls them back?

3. **Add one offer at a time.** Do not build a 12-step funnel on paper. Add the highest-impact missing piece first: usually an attraction offer if you need leads, an upsell if AOV is too low, or continuity if cash flow is unstable.

4. **Calculate the model.** Track average order value, take rate on each upsell, downsell conversion, continuity attach rate, churn, and lifetime value. The model is not real until the math is real.

5. **Remove offers that don't advance the outcome.** If an upsell makes money but hurts trust, cut it. If a continuity offer keeps people paying but does not keep helping, it will churn and poison the brand.

## Examples

**Situation:** A course creator sells a $997 course. Ads break even and then stall.

**Application:** Build the model. Attraction offer: a $29 implementation checklist with templates. Core offer: the $997 course. Upsell: a $2,500 done-with-you implementation sprint. Downsell: a $297 template pack for people who decline the course. Continuity: $99/month office hours and accountability. Winback: quarterly "implementation week" for former buyers.

**Illustrative result:** The business no longer depends on the first $997 purchase alone. Some customers enter cheaply, some buy help, some stay monthly, and former buyers have a reason to come back.

**Situation:** A service business charges $5,000 for a project and then waits for referrals.

**Application:** Attraction offer: a $500 audit. Core offer: the $5,000 implementation. Upsell: ongoing optimization for $2,000/month. Downsell: a self-serve implementation plan for $1,000. Continuity: monthly reporting and review. Winback: annual re-audit.

**Illustrative result:** The audit lowers buying friction, the implementation creates the result, and continuity turns one-time projects into predictable revenue.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Add offers before the core offer works.
**Why:** If the center is broken, the sequence just creates more ways to disappoint customers. Fix the core first.

**Don't:** Build a random funnel.
**Why:** A money model is a sequence of logical next steps, not a pile of products. If each step doesn't make the next step more obvious, the model is noise.

**Don't:** Judge the front-end offer by front-end profit alone.
**Why:** An attraction offer may be break-even or even slightly negative if it reliably creates profitable downstream buyers. Judge it by the whole model.

**Don't:** Hide bad unit economics behind complexity.
**Why:** If the model only works in a spreadsheet, it doesn't work. Track real take rates, refund rates, delivery costs, churn, and payback period.
