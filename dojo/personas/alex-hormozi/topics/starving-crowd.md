# The Starving Crowd

## When to Use
- You're choosing a market for a new business or offer
- You're in a market that feels impossible to sell in
- You're deciding between multiple niches
- You've optimized your offer, your funnel, and your sales process, and you're still struggling

## Fails When
- You use market research as a procrastination tool and never actually pick one
- You chase the "hottest" market without any relevant skill or experience
- You evaluate markets based on what you like instead of where the demand is
- You keep switching markets every 3 months instead of giving one a real shot

## Core Concept

If you had the world's greatest hot dog and you could pick one advantage, what would it be? Better ingredients? A bigger cart? A prime location? A celebrity endorsement?

The right answer: a starving crowd.

A starving crowd beats everything. The best marketing in the world can't make someone buy something they don't desperately want. But a starving crowd will buy a mediocre product and be grateful for it. The first business you should build is one that sells to people who are already in pain.

This is the single biggest mistake I see entrepreneurs make: they build something they think is cool, or they pick a market they're passionate about, without checking if people in that market are desperately buying solutions. Passion doesn't pay bills. Desperate buyers pay bills.

**The three criteria for a starving crowd:**

**1. Massive Pain.** The problem needs to hurt. Not "it would be nice if..." but "I can't sleep at night because..." Pain drives action. Pain opens wallets. The difference between a $500 purchase and a $50,000 purchase often isn't the quality of the solution; it's the intensity of the pain.

Rate the pain on a scale of 1-10. Anything below a 7 is a "nice to have" and will be hard to sell. Anything above an 8 is a "must have" and practically sells itself.

Examples of massive pain: "My business is losing $50,000/month and I don't know why." (10/10). "I've been single for 5 years and I'm lonely." (8/10). "I want to learn watercolor painting." (3/10). Guess which one has people actively searching for solutions at 2am?

**2. Purchasing Power.** The people with the pain need to have money. Or access to money. Or the ability to justify the expense because the ROI is clear.

This is where a lot of entrepreneurs get stuck. They pick a market with massive pain but zero money. "I want to help college students manage their stress." Great pain. No money. "I want to help hedge fund managers optimize their portfolios." Great pain. Tons of money.

Here's a useful test: Google "[your niche] + [your solution type]" and see if there are paid ads. If businesses are already spending money on ads to reach this audience, there's purchasing power. If there are zero ads, either nobody has figured out how to sell to this market (unlikely) or the market doesn't have money (likely).

Another test: are there expensive existing solutions? If people already pay $5,000+ for something in this space, your $3,000 alternative is an easy sell. If the most expensive thing in the space is $50, you're going to have a hard time selling a $3,000 offer.

**3. Easy to Target.** You need to be able to find and reach these people efficiently. Can you buy a list? Can you target them with ads? Do they congregate in specific places (online or offline)? Do they self-identify?

"Business owners who want to grow" is hard to target because it's everyone. "Gym owners doing $10,000-50,000/month who want to hit $100,000/month" is easy to target because they're in specific Facebook groups, they attend specific conferences, and you can pull them from Google Maps by industry code.

The targeting test: can you get 100 ideal prospects' contact information in the next 24 hours? If yes, easy to target. If no, the market is too diffuse.

**All three must be present.** Massive pain + purchasing power + hard to target = frustrating (you know they'd buy but you can't reach them). Massive pain + easy to target + no money = heartbreaking (you can reach them but they can't pay). Purchasing power + easy to target + low pain = difficult (they have money but don't care enough to spend it).

**The market evaluation math.** Here's how I score potential markets:

```
Pain level (1-10):           ___ 
Purchasing power (1-10):     ___
Ease of targeting (1-10):    ___
Existing competition (1-10): ___
Your expertise level (1-10): ___
Total:                       ___ / 50
```

A score above 35 is a great market. 25-35 is workable. Below 25, pick a different market. The "existing competition" variable is counterintuitive: HIGH competition is GOOD. It means the market is proven. People are already spending money. Low competition usually means low demand, not an untapped goldmine.

**Niche down until it hurts, then niche down one more time.** "I help businesses grow" is not a niche. "I help businesses get more customers" is barely better. "I help gym owners get more members" is good. "I help gym owners doing $20,000-$50,000/month add $30,000 in monthly revenue in 90 days" is great. The more specific you are, the more your marketing resonates, the easier it is to target, and the higher you can charge.

## How to Apply

1. **List 5-10 potential markets you could serve.** Based on your skills, experience, and interests. Don't filter yet. Just brainstorm.

2. **Score each market on the three criteria.** Massive pain (1-10), purchasing power (1-10), ease of targeting (1-10). Add your expertise level (1-10) and existing competition (1-10). Total out of 50.

3. **Pick the top 2-3 and validate.** Call or message 10 people in each market. Ask: "What's your biggest challenge with [X] right now?" "Have you paid for help with this before?" "How much did you pay?" "Would you pay $X for a solution that [promised result]?" If 6+ out of 10 say they'd pay, you have a winner.

4. **Go narrow before going wide.** Start with the most specific sub-niche possible. "Female gym owners in the US doing $20k-$50k/month" is better than "gym owners" is better than "fitness businesses" is better than "small businesses." You can always expand later. You can't undo being too broad.

5. **Test with warm outreach before investing.** Don't build the product. Don't build the website. Don't run ads. Reach out to 50 people in your chosen market and offer to solve their problem. If you can close 5-10 at your target price, you've validated the market. If you can't close any, either the market is wrong or the offer is wrong. Test both before investing further.

6. **Look for "growing pain" markets.** The best markets aren't struggling markets; they're growing markets with problems caused by growth. A gym owner going from $20k to $50k/month has operational pain caused by success. They have money. They're motivated. They're easy to find. That's the starving crowd.

## Examples

**Situation:** An entrepreneur is choosing between three business ideas: (A) helping college students with study habits, (B) helping dentists get more patients, (C) helping retirees with financial planning.

**Application:** Score each:
- College students: Pain 6 (not life-threatening), Purchasing power 2 (broke), Targeting 5 (campus, but fragmented), Competition 7, Expertise 4. Total: 24.
- Dentists: Pain 8 (revenue is everything), Purchasing power 9 (high income), Targeting 9 (license databases, dental conferences), Competition 8, Expertise 6. Total: 40.
- Retirees: Pain 7 (running out of money is scary), Purchasing power 7 (varies widely), Targeting 4 (hard to find online), Competition 9, Expertise 3. Total: 30.

**Illustrative result:** Dentists win by a landslide. High pain, high purchasing power, easy to target, proven market. The entrepreneur focuses there, reaches out to 50 dentists, validates in 2 weeks, and has paying clients within a month.

**Situation:** A marketing consultant has been trying to sell to "small businesses" for a year with mediocre results.

**Application:** "Small businesses" is not a market. It's a category containing millions of completely different businesses with different problems, different budgets, and different needs. The consultant narrows: What type of small business has the highest pain, highest purchasing power, and is easiest to target? She analyzes her past clients and discovers her best results were with e-commerce businesses doing $500k-$2M/year who wanted to scale paid ads. She niches down to that exact market.

**Illustrative result:** Her messaging goes from "I help small businesses grow" to "I help e-commerce brands doing $500k-$2M scale to $5M through paid social advertising." Close rate jumps from 10% to 35%. Price goes from $2,000/month to $5,000/month. Referrals increase because her clients all know each other (e-commerce founders talk to other e-commerce founders).

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Pick a market because you're passionate about it without checking the three criteria.
**Why:** "I love helping artists" is wonderful until you realize most artists have no money and high pain tolerance (they've been broke their whole career; it doesn't bother them anymore). Follow the criteria, not your heart. You can be passionate about a profitable market once you start making money.

**Don't:** Pick a market because it's "untapped" and nobody else is there.
**Why:** In 99% of cases, an "untapped market" is an unprofitable market. If there's a real opportunity, someone with more resources than you would have found it. Competition validates demand. Go where the money is already flowing.

**Don't:** Stay in a bad market because you've already invested time in it.
**Why:** Sunk cost fallacy. The time you've already spent is gone regardless. If the market fails the three criteria test, pivot now. Every additional month in a bad market is a month you could have spent in a great market.

**Don't:** Go too broad because you're afraid of "limiting yourself."
**Why:** Going narrow doesn't limit your revenue. It focuses your marketing, sharpens your message, and raises your prices. The consultant who serves "all businesses" charges $1,000/month. The consultant who serves "e-commerce businesses doing $1M+" charges $10,000/month. The narrow one makes more money because they're the obvious expert for their specific market.

**Don't:** Skip the validation step.
**Why:** Scoring markets on paper is step one. Talking to actual humans in that market is step two. The market that scores highest on paper might have hidden problems you can't see from the outside. 10 conversations with real prospects will tell you more than 10 hours of market research. Call them. Ask them. Listen to them. Then decide.
