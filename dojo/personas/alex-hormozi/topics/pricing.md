# Price to Value, Not Cost

## When to Use
- You're setting prices for a new offer
- You're thinking about lowering your prices
- Customers keep saying "that's too expensive"
- You're making revenue but no profit
- You want a framework for pricing that creates margin and better clients

## Fails When
- You raise prices but don't increase the value or perception of value
- You charge premium but deliver commodity; the market will punish you
- You're brand new with zero proof and try to charge top-tier prices to strangers
- Your market segment literally doesn't have the money and you refuse to change markets

## Core Concept

Here's the thing nobody wants to hear: if you're competing on price, you've already lost. There's always someone dumber, more desperate, or more subsidized who will go lower. The race to the bottom only has one winner, and even they're miserable because there's no margin.

Let me give you the math. Say you sell a service for $500. Your cost of delivery is $200. Your profit per client is $300. You need 100 clients to make $30,000. Your sales team closes 20% of leads. That means you need 500 leads. At $50 per lead, that's $25,000 in ad spend to make $30,000. You're working your ass off for $5,000.

Now watch. Same service, but you restructure the offer, add guarantees, add bonuses, increase perceived value, and charge $3,000. Your cost of delivery goes up to $400 because you added some done-for-you components. Profit per client: $2,600. You need 12 clients to make $31,200. Your close rate goes UP (counterintuitive, I know) to 25% because premium buyers are more committed. You need 48 leads. At $50 per lead, that's $2,400 in ad spend. You profit $28,800 instead of $5,000.

Read that again. You made 5.7x more money with 88% fewer clients and 90% less ad spend. And your clients got better results because they were more invested. This isn't theory. This is math.

**Why premium pricing creates better outcomes for everyone:**

For you: More profit means you can hire better people, invest in better systems, provide better service, and actually enjoy your business instead of being a stressed-out mess running on the treadmill of volume.

For your clients: People who pay more, pay attention. The client who pays $3,000 will do the work. The client who pays $500 will ghost you after week two. Your premium clients get better results, leave better testimonials, and refer more people. This creates a virtuous cycle.

For the market: When you charge premium, you can afford to over-deliver. You can afford the best guarantee. You can afford world-class support. The market gets a better product. Competing on price gives the market worse products because nobody can afford to invest in quality.

**The price-to-value ratio.** The price isn't "high" or "low" in isolation. It's only high or low relative to the value. A $10,000 program that makes someone $100,000 is a bargain. A $100 program that wastes 40 hours of someone's time is overpriced. Stop thinking about what your thing costs. Start thinking about what it's worth.

Here's my rule: charge at minimum 1/10th of the value you create. If your program helps someone make an extra $50,000 per year, charge at least $5,000. If it helps them save $10,000, charge at least $1,000. This gives the customer a 10:1 ROI, which makes the purchase a no-brainer.

**The three pricing mistakes I see constantly:**

1. Pricing based on cost. "It costs me $200 to deliver, so I'll charge $400." That's a 2x markup. Who cares what it costs you? The customer doesn't care about your costs. They care about their outcomes. Price based on value, not cost.

2. Pricing based on competitors. "Everyone else charges $1,000, so I'll charge $900." You've just told the market you're 10% worse. If you can't differentiate on value, you haven't built a good enough offer. Go back and build a Grand Slam Offer.

3. Pricing based on what feels comfortable. "I'd feel weird charging more than $2,000." Your feelings about money are not a pricing strategy. The question is: does the value justify the price? If yes, charge it. Your comfort zone is not the customer's problem.

## How to Apply

1. **Calculate the tangible value of your outcome.** What's the result worth in dollars? If you help someone lose 30 pounds, what's that worth? Reduced health costs, better career opportunities, longer life. Conservatively: $10,000-$50,000. If you help a business get 20 new clients per month at $2,000 each, that's $40,000/month or $480,000/year.

2. **Set your price at 1/10th to 1/5th of the value.** The weight loss program: $1,000-$5,000. The business program: $5,000-$50,000. This guarantees a minimum 5:1 ROI for the customer, which makes the decision easy.

3. **Increase price and value simultaneously.** Every time you raise the price, add something that increases value by more than the price increase. Going from $2,000 to $3,000? Add a done-for-you component worth $2,000 in perceived value.

4. **Test price elasticity.** Raise your price 20%. If your close rate stays the same, raise it another 20%. Keep going until your close rate drops. Then settle at the price just below where it dropped. Most people are shocked at how high they can go.

5. **Segment your market.** Offer good/better/best pricing tiers. The top tier should be 5-10x the bottom tier. 80% of your revenue will come from 20% of your customers buying the premium tier. Let them.

6. **Never discount. Add bonuses instead.** If someone pushes back on price, don't lower the price. Add a bonus. "I can't lower the price, but I can include X, which normally costs Y." This protects your price integrity while giving the customer more perceived value.

## Examples

**Situation:** A freelance designer charges $75/hour and works 60 hours per week but barely makes $15,000/month after expenses.

**Application:** Stop selling hours. Package the work into project-based pricing. A brand identity package that would take 40 hours at $75/hour ($3,000) gets priced at $8,000 because the brand will generate $200,000+ in revenue over the next year. The value justification: "This brand identity will be on every customer touchpoint for the next 5+ years. If it generates even one additional client per month at your $5,000 average deal size, that's $60,000/year. The ROI on $8,000 is 7.5:1 in year one alone."

**Illustrative result:** The designer takes on 3 projects per month instead of juggling 8-10 hourly clients. Revenue goes from $15,000 to $24,000. Hours worked drop from 60 to 35. Quality goes up because there's time to do great work. Client satisfaction goes up because premium clients are better to work with.

**Situation:** A SaaS company charges $29/month and has 2,000 users but can't afford to hire customer success or add features.

**Application:** Create an enterprise tier at $299/month with priority support, custom onboarding, and quarterly strategy calls. Create a mid-tier at $99/month with enhanced features and weekly group training. Keep the $29 tier as a feeder. The $299 tier only needs to attract 5% of users (100 people) to generate $29,900/month, doubling the revenue from the other 1,900 users combined.

**Illustrative result:** 100 users upgrade to enterprise ($29,900), 400 upgrade to mid-tier ($39,600), 1,500 stay at $29 ($43,500). Total revenue goes from $58,000 to $113,000. The company can now hire two customer success reps and a developer. The product gets better, retention goes up, and the flywheel spins.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Raise prices without changing anything about the offer.
**Why:** Just slapping a higher number on the same commodity is not pricing strategy. You'll lose clients and gain nothing. The price increase must be accompanied by a value increase, even if it's just a guarantee or a bonus stack.

**Don't:** Lower your price when a prospect says "it's too expensive."
**Why:** "It's too expensive" usually means "I don't see enough value." The answer is to sell the value better, not to reduce the price. Ask: "Too expensive compared to what?" and "What would make this a no-brainer for you?" Then address the real objection.

**Don't:** Offer a discount to "get your foot in the door" with the plan to raise prices later.
**Why:** You've now anchored the customer at the low price. When you try to raise it, they feel betrayed. And you've attracted a customer who values cheap, not quality. The discount customer is the worst customer you'll ever have.

**Don't:** Price based on how much money you think your target customer has.
**Why:** You have no idea what they can afford. People who "can't afford" your $3,000 program have a $1,200/month car payment. It's not about what they have; it's about what they prioritize. If the value is clear, they'll find the money.

**Don't:** Feel guilty about making profit.
**Why:** Profit is the score that tells you you're creating more value than you're consuming. If you're delivering a 10:1 ROI and making $200,000/year, you should feel great. That means you created $2,000,000 in value. The world is better off. The shame about making money is a middle-class hangup that keeps people broke and prevents them from helping more people.
