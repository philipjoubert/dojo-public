# Attraction Offers

## When to Use
- Prospects don't trust you enough to buy the main offer
- You need a low-friction first step into the money model
- You want better leads than a generic free download creates
- You want to prove value before asking for the bigger purchase

## Fails When
- It is just a cheap version of the main offer
- It creates delivery work that does not lead to the core sale
- It attracts people who will never buy the core offer
- It is priced or positioned so low that it cheapens the brand

## Core Concept

An attraction offer is the first step in the money model. It gets the customer to raise their hand, trust you, and often make a small purchase before the core offer. The point is not to make all the money on the first offer. The point is to create a customer who is now warmer, more qualified, and closer to the main transformation.

The best attraction offers are complete solutions to narrow problems. They do not vaguely "educate." They solve one thing. A calculator. A diagnostic. An audit. A checklist. A low-ticket workshop. A paid trial. A free-plus-shipping book. A narrow service sprint. The customer should finish it and think: this person knows what they are doing.

This overlaps with lead magnets, but the money model lens is stricter. A lead magnet asks, "Will this get me a lead?" An attraction offer asks, "Will this create the right next purchase?" A free guide that creates an email address but no buying intent is weaker than a $29 diagnostic that naturally leads to a $3,000 implementation.

## How to Apply

1. **Start from the core offer.** What must the customer believe before they buy the main thing? What small problem appears immediately before the main problem?

2. **Solve that small problem completely.** If the core offer is bookkeeping, the attraction offer might be a "profit leak audit." If the core offer is fitness coaching, it might be a 7-day meal plan. If the core offer is software implementation, it might be a workflow diagnosis.

3. **Choose free, low-ticket, or paid based on friction.** Free creates volume. Low-ticket filters seriousness. Paid diagnostics create the strongest buyers but require more trust.

4. **Build the bridge.** The attraction offer must reveal the next problem. "Now that you know where profit leaks, the next step is fixing the system that creates them."

5. **Measure downstream conversion.** The metric is not just opt-ins or purchases. Track attraction-offer buyers who buy the core offer, their refund rate, and their lifetime value.

## Examples

**Situation:** A marketing agency wants $8,000 retainers, but cold calls stall because prospects don't trust them.

**Application:** Create a $300 "customer acquisition audit." Review the prospect's funnel, show the three biggest leaks, and estimate the cost of each leak. End with the implementation offer that fixes the leaks.

**Illustrative result:** The agency stops pitching abstract marketing. It sells from a concrete diagnosis. The audit filters serious prospects and creates a natural bridge to the retainer.

**Situation:** A SaaS onboarding consultant sells a $5,000 implementation package.

**Application:** Offer a $99 onboarding teardown. Record a 15-minute review of the customer's current onboarding flow, identify friction points, and give a prioritized checklist.

**Illustrative result:** The teardown proves competence and shows the buyer the size of the problem. The core implementation becomes the obvious next step.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Make the attraction offer too broad.
**Why:** Broad offers don't demonstrate expertise. Narrow wins create trust.

**Don't:** Use an attraction offer that doesn't lead anywhere.
**Why:** Free value without a next step is charity. The buyer should naturally see why the core offer exists.

**Don't:** Underprice high-touch work.
**Why:** If the attraction offer requires your best people for hours and doesn't create core buyers, it becomes a fulfillment tax.

**Don't:** Confuse cheap with attractive.
**Why:** The attraction comes from obvious value and low risk, not merely a low price. A $500 audit can be more attractive than a free generic guide if it solves a real problem.
